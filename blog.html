Can a Brand Be Irreverent Without Making Its Product Feel Less Serious?

Looking at Columbia Sportswear's advertising journey from ‘One Tough Mother’ to ‘Engineered for Whatever’.

5 minute read
Brand strategy Advertising Sports marketing
By Abhijit Deb

Outdoor advertising can sometimes feel predictable. Mountains. Perfect equipment. Insane athletic ability. Dramatic music and someone looking heroically into the distance. We’ve seen it all, multiple times.

So when Columbia Sportswear launched the Engineered for Whatever campaign in 2025, it made sure that it stood out. Like, really made sure.

The visuals will strike you immediately. The campaign is loud, absurd (to say the least) and irreverent. Crocodiles, snowballs, snowplows and ridiculous outdoor situations replace the usual picture-perfect version of nature.

But the campaign also made me wonder: Why would a brand known for its technical outdoor gear choose this tonality for its branding? Wouldn’t it risk undermining the seriousness associated with the products it sells?

For someone who hikes and treks regularly, I learnt to appreciate quality equipment the hard way. Back in 2015, while I was camping in ‘Ilaka’, a peak in the Dhauladhar ranges of the Himalayas, I couldn’t get a wink of sleep for three consecutive nights due to a poor choice of clothing and sleeping bags. For more serious adventure activities, I could imagine equipment being the deciding factor between life and death. But let’s hold that thought for a bit.

Columbia has actually been doing this for decades.

In 1984, it introduced its One Tough Mother campaign, built around its then-president Gert Boyle and her son Tim. Gert became the demanding boss who subjected Tim to increasingly ridiculous product tests, including car washes, ice rinks and other situations designed to demonstrate the toughness of Columbia’s gear.

The humour was absurdity at its best. But more importantly, it had a job to do: product demonstration. Gert was never making fun of the jacket. She was always trying to emphasise: Look how much punishment this thing can take.

In the later years, the idea evolved, but the irreverent DNA didn’t disappear.

In 2015, Columbia brought Gert back for Tested Tough. This time, the idea expanded beyond Tim Boyle to Columbia employees testing products in harsh conditions.

Over the following years, Columbia continued experimenting with different ways of making technical outdoor performance more entertaining. Go Out Anyway, for example, used choreography, music and humour to communicate the pleasure of getting outside in cold weather. The irreverence toned down a bit during these years.

Then, Columbia turned the volume up.

In 2025, Engineered for Whatever made the irreverence much more central.

Columbia described it as its first major brand refresh in a decade and explicitly connected it to the spirit of its advertising from the 1980s and 1990s. The company also said outdoor advertising had become a “sea of sameness”, dominated by pristine landscapes and idealised outdoor imagery.

Their answer to this was unrestrained non-conformity. Instead of perfect conditions, chaos. Instead of heroic outdoor imagery, absurdity. And this definitely gave Columbia a recognisable voice again.

The campaign was created by adam&eveDDB London, an agency known for its distinctive and often unconventional storytelling, including its work for John Lewis.

Of course, irreverent advertising isn’t new. Old Spice’s iconic The Man Your Man Could Smell Like campaign, created by Wieden+Kennedy in 2010, is a great example of how absurd humour can become central to a brand’s identity. But there’s an important distinction. For a grooming brand, humour can be the product’s biggest selling point. But is it the best choice for Columbia?

Perhaps the answer lies beyond advertising. Columbia’s ACCELERATE strategy from 2024 has focused on attracting a younger, more active consumer while retaining the trust of its existing customer base. Seen in that context, Engineered for Whatever starts to make more sense. The absurdity gives Columbia a way to make the brand feel younger, more distinctive and more culturally relevant.

And as a Creative Director, it’s a direction I would be inclined to support. But I would also try to strike a balance between the two audiences. I wouldn’t remove the absurdity, but I would create a sharper contrast between the humour and the product performance. Let the ad be ridiculous. Then, when we show the product, remind people why Columbia has earned its credibility in the first place.

For example:

While the snowplow test is underway, I would cut to ultra-close-up, ultra-high-speed shots of the Glacier Ridge jacket. Show the material reacting to the snow, the construction of the jacket and the technology that allows it to withstand these conditions. Give the technical enthusiast something to believe in. Then cut straight back to the absurdity again.

To me, that’s the balance Columbia needed to strike.

Ultimately, you may like or dislike Columbia’s Engineered for Whatever campaign, but there’s a good chance that you won’t easily forget it. And perhaps that’s exactly what Columbia wanted to do.

The more interesting question isn’t whether Columbia should have taken this route. It’s how far it can take the brand into the future without diluting the legacy it has built over the years.
